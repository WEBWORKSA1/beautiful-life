Many people wait for purpose to arrive like a lightning bolt. For most of us it doesn't work that way. Purpose is usually built gradually, through experiments, reflection and commitment. Here's a practical framework to start building yours.

The four questions

Inspired by the Japanese concept often described as ikigai — loosely, "a reason for being" — this framework looks for overlap between four areas:

What do I love?

Activities that absorb you so completely that you lose track of time.

What am I good at?

Skills others come to you for, or that you develop faster than most.

What does the world need?

Problems you notice and care about, from your street to the planet.

What can I be valued for?

Ways your contribution can be rewarded — financially or otherwise.

Step 1: Collect evidence, not answers

For two weeks, note moments when you feel energised and moments when you feel drained. Patterns appear quickly. Energy is information.

Step 2: Clarify your values

Choose your top five values from a list such as: freedom, family, creativity, security, adventure, service, learning, faith, health, excellence, community, beauty. Then rank them. Your purpose will feel right only if it honours your top values.

Step 3: Run small experiments

Instead of quitting your job to "find yourself", test ideas cheaply: volunteer for a weekend, take a short course, start a side project, interview someone who does work you admire. Each experiment either moves you closer or crosses an option off the list. Both are progress.

Step 4: Write a working purpose statement

Try this template: "I use my [strengths] to [action] for [people or cause] so that [outcome]." Example: "I use my patience and organisation to help older adults feel safe and dignified." Treat it as a draft you'll revise every year.

Purpose is not a job title. It's the thread that connects what you do to why it matters.

Purpose outside of work

Not everyone finds purpose in paid work, and that's fine. Raising children, caring for a parent, creating art, building community or practising faith are deep sources of meaning. A job can fund your purpose without being your purpose.
