As children, friendships form almost automatically: you see the same people every day, with time to spare and few responsibilities. As adults, those conditions disappear. If you've found it hard to make close friends after school, university or a move, you're not alone — and it isn't a personal failing. It's a structural problem with a practical solution.

Friendship runs on time

A study by communication researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated that it takes roughly 50 hours of time together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, around 90 hours to become a friend, and more than 200 hours to become a close friend. The exact numbers vary, but the principle is clear: closeness is built through repeated time together.

The three conditions for friendship

  1. Proximity: seeing the same people regularly.
  2. Repeated, unplanned interaction: chances to bump into each other without organising it.
  3. A setting that encourages openness: where people can relax and share.

The fastest route to adult friendship is to recreate these conditions deliberately.

Six practical moves

  • Join something recurring: a weekly class, a running club, a choir, a volunteer shift. Recurrence beats one-off events.
  • Be the initiator. Most people are waiting to be invited. Suggest coffee or a walk.
  • Follow up within a week after meeting someone you click with.
  • Turn activities into rituals — a monthly dinner or a Sunday walk — so you don't renegotiate every time.
  • Share a little more each time. Closeness grows through gradual, mutual openness.
  • Revive dormant ties. Old friends and former colleagues are often the fastest path to closeness.
This week: message one person you've lost touch with. Something simple works: "Saw this and thought of you — how have you been?"

Maintaining friendships

Friendships fade mostly through neglect, not conflict. A light rhythm of check-ins — a message every couple of weeks, a meetup every month or two — keeps them alive. Put birthdays and important dates in your calendar.
