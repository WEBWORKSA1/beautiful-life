If someone sold a product that improved mood, sharpened focus, supported immunity and helped you look more rested, it would be the best-selling item on earth. It already exists, it's free, and most of us don't get enough of it. Health authorities generally recommend that adults get at least seven hours of sleep a night, yet a large share of adults regularly fall short.

Why sleep matters so much

During sleep the body repairs tissue, consolidates memories and regulates hormones involved in appetite and stress. After short sleep, people tend to be more irritable, find it harder to concentrate and make riskier choices. Over the long term, chronic short sleep is associated with a range of health problems.

The seven habits with the biggest payoff

  1. Keep a consistent wake time — even on weekends. Consistency anchors your body clock more than bedtime does.
  2. Get morning daylight to strengthen your natural day–night rhythm.
  3. Cut caffeine after early afternoon. Caffeine can stay in your system for many hours.
  4. Make your room cool, dark and quiet. Blackout curtains and an eye mask are among the cheapest upgrades.
  5. Create a wind-down buffer of 30–60 minutes: dim lights, no work email, something calming.
  6. Go easy on alcohol before bed. It may help you fall asleep, but it tends to fragment sleep later in the night.
  7. Get up if you can't sleep. After about 20 minutes awake, go to another room and do something calm until you feel sleepy.
Sleep cycles: sleep moves through cycles of roughly 90 minutes. Waking at the end of a cycle often feels easier than waking in the middle of deep sleep. Use our sleep calculator to plan around them.

Shift work and irregular schedules

If you work nights, protect your main sleep block like an appointment: blackout curtains, earplugs or white noise, phone on do-not-disturb, and a short wind-down routine even after a morning finish. Wearing sunglasses on the drive home after a night shift can help your body prepare for sleep.

When to get help

Loud snoring with gasping, persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep for months, or extreme daytime sleepiness are reasons to speak with a doctor. Sleep disorders are common and treatable. This article is for general information and is not medical advice.
