"Money can't buy happiness" is half true. Research shows that financial security reduces stress, and that higher income is generally linked with higher life satisfaction. But research also shows that two people with the same income can get very different amounts of wellbeing from it, depending on how they spend. Here are five principles that help each dollar do more.

1. Buy experiences more than things

Studies have repeatedly found that experiential purchases — trips, concerts, classes, meals with friends — tend to bring more lasting satisfaction than material ones. Experiences become part of your story, are often shared with others and are harder to compare unfavourably with what other people have.

2. Buy back your time

Research by social psychologists has found that people who spend money to save time — outsourcing a disliked chore, paying for a shorter commute — tend to report greater life satisfaction. If an hour of your time is worth more to you than the cost of a cleaner or a grocery delivery, the trade may be a good one.

3. Spend on other people

Experiments on "prosocial spending" have found that spending money on others or giving to good causes tends to boost the giver's happiness more than spending the same amount on themselves. The amount doesn't need to be large.

4. Delay consumption — and enjoy the anticipation

Looking forward to something is a pleasure in itself. Booking a holiday months in advance gives you weeks of anticipation. Paying now and enjoying later also tends to make the eventual experience feel "free".

5. Build a buffer before upgrading your lifestyle

Few purchases improve day-to-day wellbeing as much as knowing an unexpected bill won't become a crisis. An emergency fund is one of the least glamorous and most effective "happiness purchases" you can make. See our guide to building an emergency fund.

Joy-per-dollar audit: look at your last month's spending. Mark each item ★ (brought real joy or calm), ○ (neutral) or ✗ (regret). Cut the ✗s and redirect the money to more ★s.

What about big purchases?

People adapt quickly to larger homes, nicer cars and new gadgets — a process psychologists call hedonic adaptation. That doesn't mean never upgrade; it means expect the thrill to fade and make sure the purchase also solves a real, recurring problem (a shorter commute, more space for a growing family).
