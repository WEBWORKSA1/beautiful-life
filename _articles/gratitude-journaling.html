Gratitude practices are among the most studied interventions in positive psychology. In a well-known set of experiments, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked participants to keep weekly lists of things they were grateful for; compared with groups who listed hassles or neutral events, the gratitude group tended to report feeling better about their lives. Later research has explored many variations, with generally modest but meaningful benefits.

The catch: done mechanically, gratitude journaling can start to feel like a chore. Here's how to keep it fresh.

The basic method

Once a day, or a few times a week, write down three specific things you're grateful for. Some research suggests that writing a few times a week can feel fresher than writing every day, so experiment with frequency.

Five rules that make it work

  1. Be specific. "My family" is vague. "My sister texted to check how my interview went" is vivid.
  2. Focus on people more than things. Gratitude for people strengthens relationships.
  3. Include the "why". Asking why something happened links it to your choices and to other people's kindness.
  4. Notice the surprising. Unexpected good things carry more emotional weight than the routine.
  5. Vary the prompts to avoid repeating the same three items forever.

Prompts for when you're stuck

  • What made today a little easier than it could have been?
  • Which person would I miss if they disappeared from my life?
  • What did my body allow me to do today?
  • What challenge from the past am I now grateful for?
  • What did I see, hear or taste today that was beautiful?
Level up: once a month, write a short gratitude letter to someone who has helped you, and if you can, read it to them. Many people find this one of the most moving exercises in positive psychology.
